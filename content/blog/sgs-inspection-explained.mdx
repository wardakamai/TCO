A cargo of crude oil or refined product is bought and sold based on figures — a specific grade, a specific volume, a specific set of quality parameters — long before the buyer has any practical way to check those figures for themselves. Independent inspection exists to close that gap: a neutral third party verifies what was actually loaded or discharged, so neither side has to simply take the other's word for it.

## Why Independent Inspection Exists

In most commodity trades, the buyer never sees the cargo before agreeing to pay for it, and the seller is rarely present when it's discharged. Left unchecked, that creates an obvious incentive problem — a seller measuring their own cargo has no independent check on optimistic quantity figures, and a buyer relying solely on the seller's quality documentation has no protection against off-specification product. Independent inspection breaks that conflict of interest by putting a neutral, accredited third party in the loop at the point of transfer, with no financial stake in the outcome either way.

This matters just as much to banks as it does to buyers. A confirmed letter of credit issued against a cargo typically requires the shipping documents — including a Certificate of Quality and Certificate of Quantity from a recognised independent inspector — before the bank will release payment. Without that independent verification, most trade finance simply wouldn't be extended on the scale the petroleum trade operates at.

## Who Does the Inspecting

The inspection industry is dominated by a small number of globally accredited firms — SGS, Intertek, and Bureau Veritas are the three most commonly engaged in petroleum trading, alongside specialist firms like Saybolt for certain markets. All operate to internationally recognised testing standards (ASTM and ISO methods form the backbone of most petroleum inspection work) and maintain laboratory networks at or near major load and discharge ports, so testing can happen close to where the cargo actually is rather than requiring samples to travel long distances before results are available.

## What Inspectors Actually Check

**Quantity.** For cargo held in a shore tank, quantity is established through tank gauging — measuring the product level and temperature before and after a transfer, and calculating volume from the tank's calibration tables. For cargo aboard a vessel, the inspector instead conducts a draft survey: reading the ship's draft marks (how deep the hull sits in the water) before and after loading or discharge, and calculating the change in displacement, adjusted for fuel, water, and stores consumed during the operation, to arrive at cargo weight. It's a fundamentally different measurement technique from tank gauging, but a well-conducted draft survey achieves comparable accuracy for a full cargo parcel.

**Quality.** Representative samples are drawn at defined points — from the shore tank before loading, from each of the vessel's cargo tanks after loading, and sometimes as a composite sample blending proportional amounts from each tank — using standardised sampling equipment and procedures designed to avoid contamination or bias. These samples are tested against the contracted specification (EN590 parameters for diesel, ASTM D1655 for Jet A1, and so on), and a portion of every sample is sealed and retained specifically so it can be re-tested later if either party disputes the original result.

**Condition.** Beyond the numeric specification, inspectors also check for visible contamination, water content, and general cargo condition — issues that wouldn't necessarily show up as an out-of-specification lab result but would still matter to a buyer.

## The Documents That Come Out of It

A completed inspection produces several documents that travel with the cargo: a Certificate of Quality confirming the product meets its contracted specification, a Certificate of Quantity confirming the volume loaded or discharged, and an inspection report detailing the sampling and testing methodology used. Where relevant, inspectors can also verify a Certificate of Origin. Together, these form the independent verification layer that sits behind — and is checked against — the commercial documents (Bill of Lading, invoice) issued by the seller.

## When Results Are Disputed

Disputes do happen, and the system is built to handle them without relying on either party's original test. Because a sealed portion of every sample is retained specifically for this purpose, either side can call for a referee analysis — re-testing the retained sample at a mutually agreed laboratory not involved in the original inspection. The Sales and Purchase Agreement typically specifies in advance how the referee result will be treated, and what happens commercially (price adjustment, rejection, or acceptance) if the cargo turns out to be off-specification.
